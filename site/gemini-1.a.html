Important public reports exist.

They are just hard to find, hard to use, and hard to link to.

Governments publish vast amounts of high-quality information on matters of real public importance: official inquiries, investigations, and reports produced with serious resources, expert authorship, and often subpoena powers.

Yet much of this material is effectively hidden. It is scattered across obscure or broken websites, locked inside huge PDF files, poorly indexed by search engines, and almost impossible to link to at the level that actually matters — the specific finding, paragraph, or piece of evidence.

Even when journalists or commentators refer to these reports, readers are rarely taken to the report itself, let alone the relevant section. The result is that some of the most careful public research ever produced is underused in public debate.

Reports that Matter exists to fix this.

We collect, structure, and present serious reports so that they are:

  • Discoverable via search,
  • Readable online (not just downloadable PDFs),
  • Linkable at the level of specific sections or passages,
  • Easy to browse, search, and reference.

Instead of dead-end PDFs and maze-like government websites, reports become something you can actually navigate, cite, and share.

Why this is broken today

The problem is not abstract. It is structural and repeatable. Below are two representative cases, adapted directly from real government publications.

Example 1: US Senate investigation into the financial crisis

This report is one of the primary sources behind major journalism on the 2008 financial crisis — including revelations about subprime mortgage packaging and ratings-agency failures. Yet accessing it is surprisingly difficult.

What actually happens today:

  1. You must first locate the correct Senate subcommittee website (not easy).
  2. There is no simple search; you must know which Congress the report belongs to.
  3. The listing of reports itself is a PDF.
  4. That PDF contains links — some of which are broken or unreliable.
  5. Eventually, you reach multiple PDFs, split across volumes, each close to 100MB.
  6. The text versions are often unusable or incomplete.

Screenshots from the Official Inquiries deck.

At the end of this process, you may technically “have the report”, but it is still hundreds of pages long, difficult to search, impossible to link to precisely, and frustrating to use on anything other than a desktop. This is not a niche failure. It is typical.

Example 2: The Saville Inquiry (UK)

The Saville Inquiry examined the killing of civilians on Bloody Sunday — a matter of profound historical and political importance. At first glance, it looks better served. There is a bespoke website.

But once you try to use it:

  1. The “reports and statements” page contains no real links.
  2. Page numbers are plain text, not navigable.
  3. Downloads are opaque and fragmented.
  4. Alternative formats require emailing the National Archives.

Again, the issue is not the absence of information, but the absence of usable access.
