Reports that Matter
Problem Evidence Solution Scope

Important public reports exist. They are unusable on the web.

Official inquiries, investigations, and public reports carry some of the most rigorous evidence in public life. But the material is scattered, buried in PDFs, and almost impossible to link to at the level that matters.

Reports that Matter makes those reports discoverable, readable, and linkable — without turning them into a marketing product.

What we do

Make serious reports usable, not just accessible.

We collect, structure, and present public-interest reports so they can be searched, navigated, and cited. A reader should be able to reach the exact section referenced in a story, not a 700-page download.

Reports become:
  • discoverable via search
  • readable in the browser
  • linkable to specific passages
  • stable over time
Concrete examples

Evidence of a repeatable failure.

These are real government publications. The problem is not the absence of information, but the absence of usable access.

US Senate financial crisis investigation

The report behind much of the public understanding of 2008 is buried behind broken links, PDFs, and obscure committee pages. Even finding the right subcommittee site is a challenge.

Saville Inquiry (UK)

A bespoke site exists, but the reports and statements page is unusable: plain text page numbers, fragmented downloads, and requests for alternative formats by email.

Systemic framing

The pattern repeats across jurisdictions.

Important reports are published once, poorly structured for the web, and then slowly decay. Links rot. Formats become unusable. Discoverability collapses.

What changes

A small set of principles fixes the failure.

Deep links Direct readers to the exact passage that matters.
Full text Search engines can index the content properly.
Permanence Stable, web-native hosting avoids link rot.
Access Readable in the browser without massive downloads.
Why this matters

Public-interest reporting needs public-interest access.

Official inquiries often contain the factual backbone of public understanding: evidence, timelines, testimony, and findings. When this material is inaccessible, public discourse becomes thinner and less accountable.

Scope and status
